# Dig Deeper: Authentic Personal Content from an Interview

This skill guides a content creation process. It interviews the person about
a real experience, one question at a time, and turns their answers into
content, in whatever format fits their goal, keeping their voice, without
flourish and without copywriting clichés.

**Language rule (applies to every step below):** conduct the whole
interaction, interview questions and final content alike, in the language
the person is using to talk to you. This file is written in English so it is
easy to read and maintain, but none of the wording here is meant to be
copied verbatim into a conversation with someone writing in another
language. Treat every question and instruction below as a concept to adapt,
not a script to translate literally.

The final format (carousel, single post, video script, long-form text,
thread, etc.) depends on what the user asks for. If they don't say, it must
be asked before writing anything, never assumed.

## Step 1: The interview (questions adapted to the case, one at a time)

Instead of a fixed script of questions, use the bank below: 5 layers of the
story, with 4 question variants each. The point isn't to ask all 20
questions. It's to pick, for each layer, the variant that best fits what the
person already shared, and skip layers their opening message already
answered.

**Before asking anything, re-read what the person already wrote when they
brought up the topic.** If their opening message already has concrete
material for a layer (a fact, a scene, a decision), don't ask again.
Acknowledge it briefly and move to the next open layer.

### Question bank by layer

**Layer 1: The triggering fact** (what was going on)
- "Tell me about a specific time [topic] happened. What was going on at that
  moment?"
- "What led up to that moment? Was something building up before, or was it
  sudden?"
- "What was your routine or situation like right before this turned into a
  problem?"
- "Was there a specific trigger, or was it a buildup that finally hit a
  breaking point?"

**Layer 2: The concrete scene** (the point it became unsustainable)
- "Was there a specific moment, a day, a scene, when you realized it had
  reached that point? What happened (what were you doing, what did you
  feel)?"
- "What was the hardest moment of that period? What do you remember most
  vividly?"
- "Did anyone say something, or did something happen, that made you realize
  how serious it was?"
- "If you had to sum up that period in a single scene, what would it be?"

**Layer 3: The turning point**
- "How did you get out of it? Was there a decision, a change in routine,
  what pulled you out?"
- "What was different this time, compared to earlier attempts that didn't
  work?"
- "Was it one specific turning point or a gradual change? What did that
  process look like?"
- "What did you do that, looking back, actually solved it?"

**Layer 4: The lesson**
- "Looking back today, what would you do differently, or what would you tell
  someone who's exactly where you were?"
- "What was the main thing you learned from that experience?"
- "If you could go back and give yourself one piece of advice at that
  moment, what would it be?"
- "What do you understand about it now that you didn't back then?"

**Layer 5: Audience and effect**
- "Who is this content for? What do you want that person to feel by the end
  (recognition, relief, a nudge to act)?"
- "Who else might be going through something similar? What would you want
  them to know?"
- "What kind of reaction are you hoping for: a comment, a message, someone
  reaching out?"
- "Is there a specific group (a profession, a life stage, a situation) you
  picture reading or watching this?"

### How to decide how many questions to ask, and which ones

- One question at a time. Never stack more than one in the same message.
- One layer usually equals one question. Only ask a second question in the
  same layer if the answer came back too thin to yield concrete material (no
  scene, no specific fact), and even then, at most one follow-up per layer
  before moving on.
- If the person's opening message already covers a whole layer, skip that
  layer's question. Briefly confirm what you understood and move to the
  next.
- Expected result: usually somewhere between 3 and 6 questions total,
  depending on how much the person already shared up front. It's not a fixed
  target of 5, nor a requirement to ask all 20.
- After each answer, briefly acknowledge it with a short line (without
  repeating the whole answer) before the next question.

**If the person says they don't want to go into detail about something**
(most common in Layer 2, the most exposed one), respect that without
pushing, thank them, and move on. In the final content, keep the weight of
the scene without the details they asked not to expose. Usually a short, dry
line can carry the gravity without describing what happened.

## Step 2: Defining the format

If the user already named the format, any format, including one that isn't
in the examples below, use what they asked for without questioning whether
it's a "valid" option.

If they didn't, ask openly ("what format do you have in mind for this?")
before writing anything. Don't default to carousel, and don't present the
list below as if it were the only set of options. It's just a starting point
in case the person has no format in mind:

- **Slide carousel** (count and text style vary, see notes below)
- **Single post / long caption** (Instagram, LinkedIn, Facebook): the whole
  story in one block of running text, no slide breaks
- **Short video script** (Reels, TikTok, Stories): text meant to be spoken
  or narrated, with cut/scene markers
- **Long-form video script** (YouTube, a talk, a webinar): a more developed
  piece, with room for pauses, examples, and digressions a short video can't
  hold
- **Thread** (Twitter/X, LinkedIn): a sequence of short, numbered posts
- **Long-form text / article** (newsletter, blog, LinkedIn article): a more
  developed piece, with room for context and nuance that short formats don't
  allow
- **Audio/podcast script**: text meant to be spoken with no visual support,
  marked with tone and pacing instead of scene cuts
- Among others: any format the person asks for should be accepted, even if
  it isn't on this list

The story arc (hook, context, low point, turning point, lesson, closing,
described in Step 3) stays the same regardless of format. What changes is how
it's distributed: across separate slides, across paragraphs of running text,
across cuts/pauses of a spoken script, or across numbered posts in a thread.
For a format outside this list, apply the same logic: identify that format's
natural "unit" (paragraph, scene, block, speaking turn) and spread the arc
across it.

### If the format is a carousel: how many parts and what text style

Ask (or confirm, if the user already signaled it) two things before writing:

- **Number of slides**: match it to how many points the story needs, usually
  one per stage of the arc (Step 3), but nothing stops you from combining
  shorter stages into one slide or splitting a dense stage into two.
- **Text style per slide**: "tweet style" (short sentences, frequent line
  breaks, zero running paragraphs, good for a quick scroll post) is one
  option, not the only one. The person may prefer slides with a more
  developed paragraph, for instance if the content is for an audience that
  reads more slowly (LinkedIn tends to tolerate more text per slide than
  Instagram).

## Step 3: Writing the content

Arc structure that works well for this kind of story, adaptable to any
format:

1. **Hook**: the strongest scene from Layer 2 or 3, isolated, with no
   context. Whatever grabs attention right away.
2. **Context**: what caused the situation (Layer 1).
3. **The low point**: the heaviest moment (Layer 2), respecting any exposure
   limit the person set.
4. **The turning point**: the concrete decision or change (Layer 3). This
   part usually gains force precisely by contrasting with the heavy tone
   before it.
5. **The lesson**: the practical takeaway (Layer 4), without a motivational-
   speech tone.
6. **Closing**: connects the lesson to the audience defined in Layer 5. Only
   add a comment/engagement CTA if the user explicitly asks for one. By
   default, don't ask for comments, let that happen organically.

In single-block formats (single post, long-form text), these six points
become paragraphs or sentences inside the same piece, with transitions
between them, instead of separate units. In a video script, each point
becomes a spoken line/scene with a cut marker. In a thread, each point can
become one or more numbered posts.

### Style rules (apply to any format)

- **Never use the "it's not about X, it's about Z" construction.** It's a
  telltale sign of AI-generated text, and most people using this skill will
  ask you to remove it.
- **Don't repeat the same idea across different parts.** Each part should
  move the story forward, not reinforce the previous one.
- **Avoid a poetic/dramatic opening the person wouldn't actually use** (like
  "the body shutting down"). Prefer the register the person themselves used
  in their interview answers. Reuse their own words and phrasing whenever
  possible.
- Match sentence length to the chosen format, not the other way around.
  Don't force a long, reflective piece into loose tweet-style sentences, nor
  the opposite, unless the user specifically asks for it.
- After writing, **ask if the tone feels right** before moving on to the
  caption/closing. The first draft commonly sounds more "generic-
  inspirational" than how the person actually talks, and it's worth fixing
  early.

## Step 4: The caption (when the format has one separate from the main content)

Formats like carousel, video script, or thread usually have a caption
separate from the content itself; formats like single post or long-form text
already *are* the caption, in that case, skip this step.

When there is a separate caption, it **should not summarize or repeat the
main content**, since whoever reads the caption has already seen (or is
about to see) the rest. It should add **continuity**: developing an adjacent
angle to the theme (e.g., if the main content was about the problem, the
caption can move into the practical solution or a consequence not covered
there).

- Same style rules as Step 3.
- **Don't add a default comment CTA** ("comment below," "tell me in the
  comments"). Only include one if the user asks for it.
- Relevant hashtags for the theme and the audience defined in Layer 5, at
  the end.

## Step 5: Visuals (if asked, and only when the format has a visual component)

Applies to carousel, video/Stories scripts. Doesn't apply to a plain-text
single post or long-form text.

- For a "tweet style" carousel (plain text, no image), a solid background
  usually works better than stock/Pinterest images: readability issues,
  visual consistency across slides, and image rights. For a carousel with
  longer paragraphs or a slower-reading audience, a background image can
  work as long as it has an overlay to keep the text readable.
- Suggest alternating colors by emotional weight (with a solid background):
  strong colors on the highest-impact parts (hook, low point, closing),
  neutral/light color on explanatory/reflective parts. This creates a
  reading rhythm.
- If the user wants to make an exception for an image (usually on the
  cover/hook), guide them on using an overlay for text contrast, and suggest
  preferring a real photo of the person over a stock image, to keep the
  story authentic.
- For a video script, the "visual part" becomes scene/framing notes. Adapt
  the rhythm logic (impact vs. breathing room) into scene cuts instead of
  background color.

## Step 6: Final pass through the Humanizer skill

Before delivering the final version of any text produced by this skill
(main content and caption, when there is one), run the result through the
`humanizer` skill, if it's available in the environment, in **embedded
mode**: it runs its own process internally and returns just the final text,
without duplicating its rules here.

This step matters even after following the style rules in Step 3, because
those rules cover the vices most specific to this kind of content (the "it's
not about X, it's about Z" cliché, tone that doesn't match the person,
repetition across parts). Humanizer covers a broader layer of AI-writing
tics (excessive em dashes, "rule of three," promotional language,
superficial "-ing" phrases, etc.) that can slip through even in text that
already sounds like the person.

If the `humanizer` skill isn't available in the environment where this skill
is being used, skip this step without blocking delivery. The style rules in
Step 3 remain the minimum baseline.

## General notes

- Always write the final content in the language the person is using to
  talk to you. This applies to the interview questions too, not just the
  final content: adapt the wording of every question in Step 1 to that
  language rather than asking in English by default.
- After any edit, always ask if it matched their tone. Don't assume you got
  it right the first time. The person's voice is the final product, not
  yours.
- Never lock the person into one format just because it's the most common
  one. Every experience, and every social network, can call for a different
  format.
